There is a hole in this page. It is not a picture of one. Every pixel behind this sentence is a photon traced backwards through the curved spacetime around a rotating black hole, until it either hits the accretion disk, crosses the event horizon, or comes from a star.

The hole has a mass, and it is spinning. Almost all the way up: the angular momentum parameter is a* = 0.999, a few parts in a thousand short of the maximum a Kerr hole can carry. That last sliver of spin is the difference between a tidy sphere of darkness and something with a flattened, off-centre shadow and a photon ring that refuses to sit still.

Sixty-two M, and four and a half degrees above the plane

The disk is not round

The accretion disk lies in the equatorial plane, and the hole's rotation drags the space around it — frame dragging — so the material does not orbit on Keplerian ellipses. Its inner edge is the innermost stable circular orbit, at 1.24 solar masses for a hole this fast, which is comfortably inside the photon orbit. You cannot see any of it.

What you can see is the light. The near edge of the disk is moving towards you at a relativistic fraction of the speed of light and is Doppler-boosted by the fourth power of the shift factor; the far edge is receding and is dimmed by the same factor in reverse. The same disk, the same physics, two completely different objects.

Twenty-six M

A ring made of light going round

Look closely at the edge of the shadow and you will find a thin, hard line. That is the photon ring: light that orbited the hole one or more times before it found a way out. It is not an artefact of the renderer — it is in the metric, and it is brighter than anything else in the frame.

Behind it, the sky itself is bent into the shadow. Stars that are behind the hole are dragged around it and stacked into arcs. The mathematics of that is Bardeen's critical curve: the boundary of the region where a ray's radial potential has a double root, the rays that orbit forever just outside the horizon.

Eleven M

The edge is not a place

There is no surface. The event horizon at 1.99 M is a null surface in a metric, and from the outside you cannot point at it. What you see is the shadow — the silhouette of a region from which nothing escapes, which is larger than the horizon and quite differently shaped, because light bends on the way to your eye.

A static clock at this radius ticks slow. The readout in the corner is that clock: dτ/dt, the proper time of an observer who is holding still against the rotation, against the coordinate time of somebody watching from far away.

Three point two M, and closing

Last bit of sky

The camera is inside the ergosphere now, where no observer can stand still, and the shadow has swollen to fill the frame. The disk is a band of light across it. There is not much left to read, which is rather the point.

Nothing about this page was faked. The geodesics are integrated in Carter time, the radial potential is carried in double-single arithmetic so that the turning points do not quantise, and the disk is a Novikov–Thorne flux with relativistic beaming. The only thing that is a choice is how much of it you can still read.
